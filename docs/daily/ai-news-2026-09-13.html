The Future of AI Infra: from Kubernetes to Agent Sandboxes — Akshat Bubna, Modal CTO
云计算是为开发者而建的，但 agents 正在改变这一切。旧的底层设施栈是为「能读文档、能推演 YAML、能看懂仪表盘」的人类设计的——出了问题，人类可以靠脑补补全缺失的上下文，但 agent 没有这种奢侈。Modal CTO Akshat Bubna 与 swyx、Vibhu 深入探讨：为什么 AI 应用不适用于传统的云假设，为什么 Kubernetes 从不是为突发性、计算密集的工作负载设计的，以及 Modal 为何正从「开发者体验」转向「Agent 体验」。节目还拆解了 Modal 的 AI 基础设施栈——无服务器函数、装饰器式基础设施、弹性推理、GPU 快照、DeFlash、投机解码等。这家公司刚完成 3.55 亿美元 C 轮融资，是从 1700 万美元 A 轮一路走到今天的「Agent 云」未来最清晰的样本。

Thibault Sottiaux @thsottiaux
Hi Astra users. A reset and a quick update on quality issues that have been posted around. Working with some of you, we have found and fixed the following issues: - Some skills written for previous models were triggering too often or preventing the model from checking its work. - An opt-in context management experiment that could cause early stops or replies to older messages. We've disabled it. Our rough estimate is that 4-5k users were affected by this experiment. - We've also removed some badly configured engines that resulted in a measured quality degradation for a long tail of traffic flowing through them. We’ve also made some more minor improvements and things should feel significantly better across the board. More consistent follow-through, better tracking of your latest message, and better checks on the work as it’s going through the motions. The examples posted and all the users who worked directly with us were incredibly useful in helping fix things quickly. Always grateful for this incredible community. And of course, a reset is also landing by midnight today.
Astra 用户们好。关于近期被贴出的质量问题，这里做一个重置与快速更新。 在与部分用户合作排查后，我们已定位并修复了以下问题： - 一些为旧模型编写的 skills 被过度触发，或阻止了模型检查自己的工作。 - 一个可选的上下文管理实验可能导致提前终止或回复旧消息，我们已将其关闭。粗略估计有 4-5 千用户受到该实验影响。 - 我们还移除了一些配置不当的引擎，它们导致流经其上的长尾流量出现可测量的质量下降。 我们还做了一些小的改进，整体体验应该会明显变好：更一致的跟进、更好地追踪你最新的消息、在执行过程中更好地检查工作。 所有被贴出的例子和直接与我们合作的用户都极有帮助，让我们能快速修复。永远感激这个不可思议的社区。 当然，重置也将在今天午夜前落地。

Matt Turck @mattturck
My 9/11 story (lightly updated version of a blog post I wrote in 2016 for the 15th anniversary) In September 2001, I was the young co-founder of a NYC tech startup, TripleHop Technologies.   We were building enterprise search software, using whatever AI existed at the time, building what would be today close to what a Glean does.  We had maybe 25 people on the team at the time. Pretty much all of us were at the very begining of our careers, barely out of school, unexperienced, but highly enthusiastic. Our office was located on the 53rd floor of the North Tower at the World Trade Center. We had ended up at the World Trade Center fairly randomly.  Our first office was located on Water Street, right above a 24 hour McDonald’s.  It looked more like a decrepit apartment than an actual office, occasionally didn’t smell that great, but it was cheap and good enough. But after a year or so, the team had grown, and that office had become too small, so we started looking for a bigger one. At the time, for a reason or another, there wasn’t a lot of office inventory on the market in the area where we wanted to be.   We looked for a bit, couldn’t find anything suitable, until one of us had the strange idea of inquiring at the World Trade Center, which felt like a long shot. Turned out that the World Trade Center had some smaller offices, and some availability.  In those dot-com bubble days, they also were quite eager to attract young tech startups, and were willing to offer us some preferential terms. So we went to take a look, and visited two offices in the North Tower. One was a large office at the very top of the building, perhaps the 104th floor, I don’t exactly recall. It had unbelievable views, but it really felt like taking that office would be pushing it – even with a good deal, we were just a little seed-funded startup, after all. Also, the elevator ride took forever. So instead we chose the smaller, cheaper office on the 53rd floor.   We really liked our new office, and the World Trade Center in general.  It felt like a little town, with a lot of retail and amenities below the surface, and its own subway station. We ate a bit too often at a Sbarro joint located right outside the ground floor elevator. The night before 9/11, my co-founder Renaud Laplanche and I worked very late into the evening, as we did most nights.  In true nerdy founder fashion, we played a ping pong game or two, as we did pretty much every time before calling it a night – the ping pong table being our attempt at creating a startup culture.   I left, and think Renaud may have stayed on that night to finish working on a proposal for a prospective customer. The morning after, when the plane hit the North Tower at 8:46am, I was home, about to leave for work.   Like many young tech people, we were night owls but not early birds, typically showing up at the office around 9:30am.   I had the TV on, and as about to turn it off and walk out of the door, saw the “breaking news” scroll at the bottom of the screen mentioning a plane collision, but no image.     As I had a faraway view of the World Trade Center from the window of my West Village studio apartment, I looked up and saw what seemed like a little bit of smoke at the top. I assumed the plane that had hit the tower was a small tourist plane.  We would often see them fly by the office, above the nearby Hudson River, often at altitudes that felt very close to us.  It had crossed my mind more than once that it wouldn’t take that much for one of them to hit the tower by accident. Then, like so many others, I saw those unbelievable images on TV. I called my family back in France.  Shortly thereafter, phone lines went down. The rest of the day was surreal and gut-wrenching, although I remember feeling mostly numb. I went on a mad scramble to see if everyone at TripleHop was ok. For many hours, we just didn’t know.  With no phone working, we accounted for most people via email, but a number did not reply for a while. It eventually turned out only one person from the team, Kenton Beerman, was at the office at 8:46am.   He made it out shocked but unscathed. Perhaps as a way to cope psychologically, or just because they were amazing people, I remember that the team was incredibly focused on saving the business that day.   This was obviously pre-AWS, we had our own server room in our office, but thankfully our CTO Joaquin Delgado and his team had backed everything up.  By the end of the day, we were essentially back up and running. After a few days, we found shared office space.  But business didn’t go back to usual. In addition to everything else, 9/11 was the final nail in the coffin for the economy after the dot com bubble burst.  The next couple of years were a complete nuclear winter. Thankfully, we had raised a $5M Series A (a solid amount at hte time!) a few months before 9/11, which kept us afloat. We also managed to win a few deals in Europe, which gave us a bit more cash.   Eventually business picked up strongly in late 2003 and 2004, potential acquirers came knocking, and TripleHop was acquired by Oracle in 2005. We all went on with our lives.  My co-founder Renaud reached extraordinary entrepreneurial success and defined a whole new industry. We all have families now.  I occasionally see pictures of beautiful children on my Facebook feed. I rarely talk about 9/11, and have tried to not think about it too much. It took me a very long time to go visit the 9/11 memorial downtown.   I finally made it just a few weeks ago, after a board meeting nearby, walking there almost by accident. Looking at the names engraved in the marble, I was reminded that it wouldn’t have taken much.  An hour later, a few floors lower. Or choosing that office on the 104th floor instead of the other one, a few months prior. And I feel deeply connected not just to those names, but to New York. Before 9/11, New York felt like an exciting, but ultimately foreign, city. After 9/11, I truly started feeling like a New Yorker. Everyone remembers where they were on 9/11.  Everyone has a story of some sort, many infinitely more tragic. This is just mine.
我的 9/11 故事（2016 年博客的轻量更新版）。 2001 年 9 月，我是纽约科技创业公司 TripleHop Technologies 的年轻联合创始人。我们做企业搜索软件，办公室在世贸中心北塔 53 层。 前一天晚上我和联合创始人 Renaud Laplanche 加班到很晚，像往常一样打了两局乒乓球。第二天早上 8:46 飞机撞上北塔时，我还在家准备出门。 那天只有一位同事 Kenton Beerman 在办公室，他受了惊吓但安然无恙。公司 CTO 提前做了备份，当天晚上我们基本恢复了运转。 9/11 成为互联网泡沫破裂后压垮经济的最后一根稻草。幸好我们在几个月前完成了 500 万美元 A 轮融资，撑了下来。2005 年 TripleHop 被 Oracle 收购。 直到几周前我才第一次去参观 9/11 纪念馆。看着刻在大理石上的名字，我意识到：差一点，可能就是我在那里。9/11 之后，我才真正感觉自己是个纽约人。

Why most enterprise AI efforts fail. 1/ Using old-school playbooks for AI The CEO picks a trusted lieutenant to lead a central AI team. That leader pulls in a group of trusted people from across the company. The problem here is the skill gap. The team structure, product patterns, how you launch and learn that got you here do not work with AI. Much of product development over the last 15 yrs has been incremental improvements. AI requires experimentation and invention. 2/ Under-investing in evals Most enterprises don't quite understand the importance of this and what good looks like here. (I have a whole evals series in the comments - check it out) 3/ Building AI for the company from the outside The central AI team declares itself a platform team and builds tools that everyone is supposed to adopt. These products are often disconnected from the actual workflows, context and judgment of the people doing the work. Begrudging adoption follows. But not meaningful productivity gains. What you should do: 1/ Hire leaders who have done successfully built AI products. You need technical/product depth plus the ability to earn trust with the people whose jobs they’re helping reinvent. 2/ Make evals a first class citizen in your AI efforts 3/ Embed your best AI builders inside the functions you want to transform. Build * with * finance , build * with * sales, build * with * support.
为什么大多数企业 AI 尝试会失败。 1/ 用老一套打法做 AI：CEO 派一位信任的副手领导中央 AI 团队，从全公司抽调信任的人。问题在于技能缺口——过去 15 年的产品开发大多是渐进式改进，而 AI 需要实验和发明。 2/ 对 evals 投入不足：大多数企业并不真正理解 eval 的重要性，也不知道好的标准是什么样。 3/ 从外部为企业构建 AI：中央 AI 团队自称平台团队，构建所有人都该采用的工具。这些产品往往与实际工作流程、上下文和一线人员的判断脱节，导致勉强采用而非实质性的生产力提升。 你应该做的：1/ 雇佣真正成功构建过 AI 产品的领导者；2/ 让 evals 成为 AI 工作的一等公民；3/ 把你最好的 AI 构建者嵌入你想变革的业务部门里，和财务、销售、支持一起构建。

“Success has many fathers, but failure is an orphan.” I never realized the gravity of this quote till I saw extremely successful VCs fight for attribution of the hot deals.
